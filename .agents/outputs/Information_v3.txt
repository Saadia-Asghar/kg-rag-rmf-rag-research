Abstract
Procedural knowledge is essential in specialized domains, and natural language tools
for retrieving procedural knowledge are necessary for non-expert users to facilitate their
understanding and learning. In this study, we focus on function decomposition trees, a
framework for representing procedural knowledge, and propose a natural language inter-
face leveraging Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). The natural language interface
converts the user’s inputs into SPARQL queries, retrieving relevant data and subsequently
presenting them in an accessible and chat-based format. Such a flexible and purpose-driven
search facilitates users’ understanding of functions of artifacts or human actions and their
performance of these actions. We demonstrate that the tool effectively retrieves actions,
goals, and dependencies using an illustrative real-world example of a function decompo-
sition tree. In addition, we evaluated the system by comparing it with ChatGPT 4o and
Microsoft GraphRAG. The results suggest that the system can deliver responses that are
both necessary and sufficient for users’ needs, while the outputs of other systems lack the
key elements and return redundant information.
Keywords: function decomposition tree; retrieval-augmented generation; large language model
1. Introduction
In the context of knowledge management applications, effective handling of procedu-
ral knowledge is considered crucial [1]. Procedural knowledge refers to the processes and
workflows that guide the execution of a specific task and is typically described in natural
language through documents such as guidelines and manuals. For example, an official
document regarding asset accounting procedures has been published by Japanese govern-
ment in the field of accounting audits [2], and adherence to these procedures is required.
Procedural knowledge can also be formalized and represented as structured knowledge
using modeling languages such as BPMN [3] and IDEF3 [4]. To facilitate understanding
and learning for non-expert users, a natural language interface is required for interacting
with these forms of procedural knowledge.
Currently, natural language interfaces for extracting procedural knowledge are clas-
sified into three categories according to the nature of the knowledge sources provided to
generate responses. The first approach relies solely on user queries as input to a general-
purpose large language model (LLM). General-purpose LLMs are models that have not

been fine-tuned for a specific domain. They have the potential to implicitly capture pro-
cedural knowledge and generate step-by-step actions from high-level task descriptions,
although the accuracy and consistency of generated procedures are limited [5]. The second
approach provides the LLMs with domain-related procedural knowledge documents. In
this approach, one method directly refers to textual documents, whereas the other indirectly
refers to them through the derivation of a knowledge graph from the text [6]. The third
approach directly provides the LLMs with a structured knowledge graph as the source
of knowledge. In this approach, the knowledge graph provided to LLMs is manually
constructed in advance. This last approach has been studied as part of a body of research
into integrating LLMs with knowledge graphs [7].
In this study, we adopt the third approach. As a method of providing structured
knowledge, we employ function decomposition trees, which are a descriptive method
originally proposed for functional knowledge of artifacts in engineering design [8]. It has
been extended to procedural knowledge of human actions called CHARM (Convincing
Human Action Rationalized Model) in the health care field [9]. One of their important
features is the explicit descriptions of the purposes or goals of actions. For example,
in accounting auditing, the function decomposition tree has been used to model audit
procedures based on auditing standards [10]. In this study, we propose a natural language
interface based on function decomposition trees.
Many studies have explored systems that allow for searching knowledge graphs
using natural language [11,12]. In the proposed interface, the schema of the function
decomposition trees is first converted into RDF, a format supported by LLMs, to enable the
model to refer to their data. Then, a SPARQL query is generated from the user’s inputs to
retrieve relevant information from the function decomposition trees, which is subsequently
used to generate the final response [13].
To illustrate the efficacy and practical utility of the proposed method, the three ap-
proaches described above were compared in an evaluation experiment. The compared
systems were (1) ChatGPT 4o, as a general-purpose LLM (the first approach), (2) Microsoft
GraphRAG (v.2.4.0) [14], which indirectly refers to additional domain-related documents
through the automatic derivation of a knowledge graph (the second approach), and (3) the
proposed system, which utilizes a manually constructed knowledge graph (the third ap-
proach). For this evaluation, accounting auditing, a specialized domain that requires the
use of procedural knowledge, was selected, and the system was evaluated through both
quantitative metrics and expert-based assessments.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the related work
on knowledge graphs and natural language interfaces; Section 3 introduces the proposed
method for developing an interface based on function decomposition trees; Section 4
presents a real-world example involving a function decomposition tree based on auditing
procedures in accounting; Section 5 presents a comparative evaluation of the proposed
method against other approaches, including Microsoft GraphRAG and a standard LLM;
and Section 6 discusses the limitations of the proposed approach and outlines directions
for future work. Finally, Section 7 concludes the paper.

functions or actions into their constituent sub-functions or sub-actions, organizing them
hierarchically in a tree-shaped knowledge graph [8,9]. Figure 1 illustrates an example of a
function decomposition tree aimed at handling a cold.

The function decomposition tree includes three fundamental node types—function
nodes, way nodes, and relation nodes—visualized as ellipses, squares, and arrow-linked rect-
angles, respectively. A function node denotes a function that can be accomplished either
by artifacts or through human activities. Meanwhile, a way node expresses a way of func-
tion/action achievement, indicating the approach used to achieve a function or an action.
Within the hierarchical tree, each function node can be expanded into multiple way nodes,
which themselves decompose into further function nodes, revealing recursive layers of
detailed action structures. As shown in Figure 1, “handling a cold” can be approached
through either the “Prophylaxis way” or the “Symptomatic way”. This hierarchical struc-
ture effectively illustrates multiple possible ways toward achieving the same objective. In
essence, the model captures the goal–method relationship underlying a series of actions.
Moreover, function nodes may include a functional category attribute that clarifies the
specific nature or role of the function or action. For instance, in Figure 1, the action “Prevent
virus invasion into the body” is categorized as a prevention function (depicted in purple),
whereas the counter functions are shown in yellow.
Relation nodes express interdependencies among way nodes, indicating cases in which
selecting one way influences other ways. For instance, in Figure 1, choosing the “mask-
wearing way” to “prevent virus invasion into the body” also satisfies the action “prevent
secondary infections”, as indicated by the relation node “interlocking selection relation.”
One of most important features of the function decomposition tree here is “goal-
oriented”-ness. In comparison with other methods for process modeling such as BPMN [3]
and IDEF3 [4], the goal of a function or an action and the ways of achievement are explicitly
represented in a hierarchy. Although the importance of a “goal” in process representa-
tion has been pointed out and a goal-oriented extension of BPMN (GO-BPMN) has been
proposed [15], the ways of achievement are implicit. In comparison with traditional func-
tion decomposition [16] and its extensions [17,18], the explicit description of the ways of
achievement is a unique feature. Thus, users’ questions to the natural language interface
developed in this research should include (1) What are the ways to achieve a goal? (2) What
is the purpose of an action? (3) Which ways are impacted when applying a way?
2.2. Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) integrates LLMs with external retrieval mech-
anisms [19–22]. Conventional LLMs are prone to hallucinations and often struggle when
dealing with queries beyond their training data, making it difficult to trace reasoning
processes. RAG mitigates these issues by leveraging external data sources to enrich the
generation process, thereby enhancing accuracy and reliability. In RAG, user queries trigger
searches in an external database, and responses are generated based on the search results.

While most RAG systems employ vector databases as the external reference, recent research
has focused on using knowledge graphs as knowledge sources [23]. Specifically, our ap-
proach focuses on retrieval from RDF-transformed function decomposition tree data, rather
than on general knowledge graphs. This approach requires translating natural language
queries into SPARQL.
Text-to-SPARQL conversion has been extensively studied [24–28]. However, prior
research has mainly addressed general-purpose RDF datasets. In contrast, the present
study concentrates on SPARQL generation over RDF data specifically designed to represent
function decomposition trees, aiming at generating answers for the three types of user
questions mentioned above.
As features of the function decomposition tree, functions or ways are described by
labels, with rdfs:label having an important meaning in the converted RDF dataset. Gen-
eral text-to-SPARQL conversion tends to emphasize the structure of the knowledge graph
and links between nodes rather than labels; it is difficult to extract important information
contained in the labels of the converted RDF dataset. In the proposed system, we extend
the RDF dataset by adding a keyword property as described later in Section 3.2 to perform
keyword-based searches, and adopt the few-shot prompting approach to generate SPARQL
queries instead of utilizing general text-to-SPARQL methods. This enables the system to
specialize the query generation process for searching the function decomposition tree as
the RDF data with the keyword property.
3. Methods
This section outlines the development of a natural language search interface for function
decomposition tree data. To achieve this, we first identified the key conceptual components of
the function decomposition tree and designed an RDF Schema tailored to them. Based on this
schema, we implemented a conversion system that transforms the tree data into an RDF dataset.
Finally, we developed a chat-based search interface that leverages RAG to enable efficient and
flexible querying of the function decomposition tree.
3.1. Components in a Function Decomposition Tree
We define two fundamental components of a function decomposition tree: functional
categories and relations. The former describe the types of functions or action nodes possess,
while the latter express dependencies between way nodes.
Nodes that represent functions within a function decomposition tree are referred to as
Systemic Processes, which are characterized by initial and end states, with the end state serving as
the goal state. Such processes can be categorized into two subclasses based on their goals: intended
goal processes and unintended goal processes. Our proposed approach incorporates both types of
processes in defining and representing function decomposition trees.
3.1.1. Functional Category
Functional categories represent different types of functional or action nodes defined
within the tree. Each functional category is treated as a specialization of function nodes.
The counter function and the prevention function as described below are defined as subclasses
of the Systemic Process.
3.1.2. Functional Category: Counter Function
A function decomposition tree may contain not only actions to be performed but also
failure events and side effects resulting from these actions. These elements are categorized
as counter functions. For instance, in Figure 1, the subtree rooted at the node labeled
“worsening of symptoms”, shown in yellow, exemplifies this type of action. By explicitly
representing failure events and side effects, the intentions behind corresponding actions

become clearer, potentially supporting defect prevention [8]. Similar to regular actions,
shown in blue, failure or side-effect nodes are hierarchically decomposed—linking higher-
level effects to lower-level causes. However, unlike normal actions, failure events or
side effects are not intended to be executed. We define counter functions as a subclass of
unintended goal processes within RDFS.
3.1.3. Functional Category: Prevention Function
Some actions in a function decomposition tree are preventive in nature and are thus
classified as prevention functions. In Figure 1, the subtree labeled “prevent a cold” exemplifies
such an action. Unlike typical actions that aim to achieve outcomes, preventive functions
are designed to avert specific undesired states.
In the context of processes aimed at prevention, we can decompose the concept of
intended goal processes into two categories: actions and functions. Actions refer to processes
executed by living organisms, while functions are carried out by inanimate entities; both
types can serve preventive purposes. Accordingly, we define prevention actions as a subclass
of actions, and prevention functions as a subclass of functions. Since their overarching concepts
of actions and functions are not explicitly distinguished within a function decomposition
tree, prevention actions and prevention functions are treated as the same type of functional
category. However, these concepts are defined as separate entities in an RDF to facilitate
more precise querying.
3.1.4. Dependencies Between Ways
In a function decomposition tree, certain ways are interdependent in terms of their
selection or activation. We classify these dependencies into two primary types: interlocking
selection relation and exclusive relation. An interlocking selection relation indicates that selecting
one way necessitates the selection of another. Conversely, the exclusive relation relationship
ensures that if one way is selected, the other is precluded. Conversely again, the exclusive
relation relationship enforces mutual exclusivity, ensuring that the selection of one way
automatically excludes the other. Exclusive relations can be further divided into sibling
relations, where the ways share the same parent node, and contradictory relations, where the
dependent ways are located in distant branches of the tree. Both interlocking selection relations
and exclusive relations are expressed through relationship nodes, defined as RDF properties
that are sub-properties of their respective dependency categories. For instance, in Figure 1,
the node connecting the two mask-wearing ways represents an interlocking selection relation. In
the proposed method, we defined the dependencies between ways by treating relationship
nodes as RDF properties. These nodes are described as sub-properties of each dependency
type, allowing for a structured representation of the dependencies within the function
decomposition tree.
3.1.5. Schema
Based on the components of a function decomposition tree defined in Section 3.1, we
developed an RDF Schema (Figure 2) representing the structure of function decomposition

label is omitted. Additionally, the descriptions of rdfs:Class and rdf:Property in some
parts of Figure 2 are also partially omitted, as their semantics can be inferred through

As we described in Section 2, rdfs:label in the converted RDF dataset has an impor-
tant meaning. However, because such queries rely on exact text matches, the presence of
semantically equivalent but lexically different expressions could lead to search failures. To
mitigate this issue, we introduced an additional:keyword property alongside rdfs:label.
Keywords are automatically extracted from each node’s rdfs:label using an LLM and
stored under the :keyword property. This extension improves retrieval robustness by
enabling semantic-level matching rather than strict lexical equality.
3.3. Searching for RDF Data Using a Chat-Based Interface with RAG
We implemented a chat-based interface that enables natural language search over
function decomposition trees. User queries are first transformed into a SPARQL query
through the application of an LLM. We employed OpenAI’s gpt-4o-mini model for this
transformation. The resulting query is executed on the RDF dataset, and the retrieved
results are reformulated into natural language responses through the same model. As
described in Section 2.1, we observed that user queries typically fall into three main
categories, each handled by a specialized prompt configuration. The following subsections
illustrate these three interaction modes using the function decomposition tree in Figure 1.
3.3.1. Decomposition of Actions and Ways
In the first pattern, the system identifies and presents specific actions or ways related
to the user’s query. This procedure begins with the extraction of keywords from a natural
language sentence provided by the user. These keywords are then utilized to perform a
filtering process on the :keyword property as described in Section 3.2. This filtering helps to
narrow down the search to identify the node that is considered to be the closest match to the
user’s input. Next, a SPARQL query is generated to search for function nodes or way nodes
that indicate the approach to achieving the identified node. The results from executing this
query are then used to construct the response. The function of this SPARQL query is to
identify sub-nodes that exist beneath a specific node within a function decomposition tree.
For example, when the user asks: What are the ways to address symptoms?, as illustrated
in Figure 3, the extracted keywords “address” and “symptom” are used to identify relevant
nodes. Subsequently, subordinate nodes under the matched node are identified and results

relief way” are returned. Finally, using these results, the system presents the following

This approach enables the system to output actions related to the identified ways if the
matched nodes are way nodes. Conversely, if the matched nodes are action nodes, the system

3.3.2. Identifying the Purpose of Actions
In the second pattern, the system infers and presents to the user a purpose or goal of an
action or function associated with a specific action or way within the function decomposition
tree. Similarly to as outlined in Section 3.3.1, after extracting keywords from the query, the
system searches for function nodes located above the matched node in the decomposition
tree. These keywords are then used to filter nodes via the :keyword property, enabling the
system to identify the node that best matches the user’s input. Subsequently, a SPARQL
query is generated to search for function nodes that are positioned above the specified node,
which serves as the goal of either function nodes or way nodes. The results of executing this
query are then used to construct the response. This SPARQL query is designed to locate
action nodes that exist above a given node within the function decomposition tree.
Let us suppose a user inputs the following, as illustrated in Figure 4: What is the purpose of
washing hands? The system extracts “wash” and “hand” as keywords and uses filtering to locate
the relevant node. It then searches for action nodes above this node in the tree (Figure 1). The
generated response is: The purpose of washing hands is to “Prevent virus invasion into the body.”
The act of washing hands has a “prevention function.” This approach allows the system to output
function nodes located above the node corresponding to the user’s input. Additionally, it can
present the type of function that the function node aims to achieve.

3.3.3. Identifying Dependencies Between Ways
In the third pattern, the system identifies and presents dependencies among ways
within the function decomposition tree to the user. The procedure begins with the extraction
of keywords from a natural language sentence provided by the user, following procedures
similar to those in Sections 3.3.1 and 3.3.2. These keywords are then used to filter nodes to
identify the way node that best matches the user’s input. Next, properties are searched for,
where the node in question serves as the subject and another way node acts as the object.
Finally, the results are utilized to generate a response. As defined in Section 3.1.5, a relation
node is characterized by the :has_Relation property, which indicates way nodes as both
the subject and predicate. Consequently, this SPARQL query is designed to search for
relation nodes associated with a particular way within a function decomposition tree. For
example, given the input: Which ways are impacted when applying the mask-wearing way?, as
illustrated in Figure 5, the system then checks if the identified way nodes have any associated
relational nodes. In this example, an “Interlocking selection” relation node exists, linked
to the “Mask-wearing way.” Using this connection, the system generates the response:
The mask-wearing way has an “Interlocking selection relation” with the “mask-wearing way” to
“Prevent secondary infections.” Therefore, when applying the “Mask-wearing way”, not only
is the goal of “preventing virus invasion into the body” achieved, but also secondary infection
prevention is significantly affected. This response clarifies that using the “mask-wearing way”
for infection prevention also helps prevent secondary infections in others. In this pattern,
the system provides the user with insights into additional outcomes that can be achieved
when selecting a specific way to accomplish a particular action.

4. Real-World Example
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, we present a real-world example
involving a function decomposition tree about auditing procedures in accounting [10].
This function decomposition tree was constructed based on the official document Asset
Accounting Procedures published by the Financial Services Agency of Japan [2]. The tree com-
prises 247 nodes, and the intricate interconnections among them make manual searching
exceedingly difficult. Because the function decomposition tree is documented in Japanese,
all searches were also conducted in Japanese. The following subsections illustrate examples
of operations for the three patterns described in Section 3.3.
4.1. Decomposition of Actions and Ways in Accounting Audit
As an example of the first interaction pattern (“Decomposition of Actions and Ways”)
described in Section 3.3.1, we demonstrate how the system searches for the ways to perform
grouping in accounting audits (Figure 6). Here, grouping refers to the process of dividing
assets into independent units.
Let us suppose that a user inputs the query: What are the ways for grouping assets? The
system identifies the relevant keywords within the query, uses them to filter nodes that
correspond to the user’s intent, and then examines the subnodes linked to these relevant
nodes to generate an answer based on the retrieved results. In this example, the generated
response is as follows: The ways to group assets include “when there is goodwill,” “when there
is no goodwill with shared assets,” and “when there are shared assets.” Here, shared assets refer
to assets that contribute to the cash flows of multiple asset groups or business units, while
goodwill denotes the difference between the acquisition price and the fair value of net assets.

4.3. Identifying Dependencies Between Ways in Accounting Audit
Finally, as an example of the third interaction pattern, “Identifying Dependencies
between Ways,” introduced in Section 3.3.3, we show how the system identifies implications
associated with selecting a specific way in the function decomposition tree (Figure 8).

system extracts keywords from the query, filters matching nodes, and examines whether
the corresponding node is connected to any relation node. If the node that matches the
user’s query has a relation node, the system searches for the node that is connected to the
relation node. In this example, the generated answer is as follows: The book value allocation
way has a “linked selection relationship” with the way of archiving the act of “determining the
grouping way for shared assets”. By choosing this way, the grouping way for assets is determined.

explicitly described. Our system ensures these implicit relationships are explicit, allowing
users to clearly understand how the selection of one way may influence others within the
decomposition structure.

5. Comparison with Other Methods
5.1. Comparison Method and Result
As discussed in Section 1, there are three types of approaches to natural language
interfaces for extracting procedural knowledge. To evaluate the proposed system, we
compared the other two approaches with the proposed system. Microsoft GraphRAG
was selected as a comparative approach as it is conceptually similar in that it utilizes
knowledge graphs, deriving them from source documents and using them to generate
responses [14]. To evaluate the performance of the system, we submitted similar questions
to both Microsoft GraphRAG and a standard LLM (ChatGPT 4o). Their responses were
then systematically compared. The system’s core and foundational pattern, involving the
decomposition of actions and ways, was applied to the questions.
The questions posed concerned the grouping of assets in accounting auditing, in alignment
with the discussion presented in Section 4.1. As we explained in Section 4.1, grouping refers to the
process of dividing assets into independent units. In order to evaluate each response, an expert in
accounting auditing evaluated the correctness and appropriateness of the responses; examined
whether the necessary information was included; and compared the results.
5.1.1. The System Using the Function Decomposition Tree
As described in Section 4.1, the system answered that: The ways to group assets include
“when there is goodwill,” “when there is no goodwill with shared assets,” and “when there are
https://doi.org/10.3390/info17010055

shared assets.” This answer explicitly indicates that the way of grouping assets depends on
the presence or absence of shared assets and goodwill.
5.1.2. Microsoft GraphRAG
To evaluate Microsoft GraphRAG, we provided the system with the same source doc-
ument [2], and it autonomously constructed a knowledge graph based on the document’s
content and subsequently generated responses by leveraging the graph structure (“local
search”). The domain expert evaluated the system’s responses to the query: “What are the
ways for grouping assets?” and provided two key observations. First, the response included
extraneous content that was not directly relevant to the question. For instance, it referred
to measuring impairment losses and considering remaining economic life. However, these
topics are not directly related to the methods of asset grouping and were therefore deemed
unnecessary to include in the response. Second, the response lacked key elements neces-
sary for a comprehensive answer. While it addressed the presence of shared assets in the
context of asset grouping, it failed to mention the treatment of goodwill, which is a critical
consideration in grouping assets.
5.1.3. A Standard LLM
The domain expert evaluated ChatGPT’s response to the query: “What are the ways
for grouping assets?.” Similarly to Section 5.1.2, the response included content that was not
directly relevant to the question. For example, statements concerning legal or contractual
constraints do not have a direct impact on asset grouping and were therefore considered
extraneous. In addition, certain statements in the response employed assertive language
that may be misleading. For example, while the response states that assets should be
grouped when they are physically or functionally related, there are cases in which such
assets are not grouped, depending on the specific circumstances. Similarly to the responses
discussed in Section 5.1.2, the LLM’s output also failed to address the treatment of goodwill,
which is one of the key elements in asset grouping.
5.2. Results and Discussion
To quantitatively evaluate the systems, we prepared a gold-standard answer and
computed the F1 score based on the gold-standard. The results are summarized in Table 1.
The gold-standard answer to the query: “What are the ways for grouping assets?” focuses
on the direct sub-actions and the ways of achievement. It consists of six items (shown as the
“Number of total correct statements” in Table 1). Next, we counted the number of output
statements generated by each system (shown as the “Number of output statements”) and
classified each statement into one of three evaluation labels: relevant, acceptable, and
irrelevant. Here, relevant indicates statements that correctly and directly correspond to
one of the statements in the gold-standard answer; acceptable denotes statements that
are partially correct or indirectly related; and irrelevant refers to statements that are
either not directly relevant to the question or factually incorrect. Based on these labels,
we computed strict precision, relaxed precision, recall, and F1 score. For strict precision,
only relevant statements were regarded as correct, whereas for relaxed precision, both
relevant and acceptable statements were considered correct.
One of the characteristics of a function decomposition tree is that it describes sub-
actions and ways to achieve actions separately. Therefore, when decomposing an action,
the proposed system first outputs the ways corresponding to the given action. In contrast,
ChatGPT and GraphRAG directly output sub-actions as the result of the decomposition.
To align the evaluation conditions, as shown in Figure 6, the proposed system was used
to generate action decompositions through users’ follow-up questions, which elicited the
actions contained in each way.

In addition, given the nature of the function decomposition tree as the underlying knowledge

direct sub-actions of the given action and the ways of achieving actions.
As shown in Table 1, ChatGPT and GraphRAG achieve lower precision than the
proposed system. As explained in Sections 5.1.2 and 5.1.3, the outputs of both Microsoft
GraphRAG and ChatGPT contained irrelevant information that is not directly related to
asset grouping methods. Such extraneous content may lead to confusion among users and
hinder their understanding of the responses. In particular, ChatGPT shows a substantially
poor strict precision, indicating that it has difficulty producing information that directly
contributes to the achievement of the given action. In addition, ChatGPT and GraphRAG
exhibited lower recall than the proposed system, suggesting that their responses lack
important concepts, such as goodwill (as explained in Sections 5.1.2 and 5.1.3), required for
a complete answer. In summary, by extracting information from the function decomposition
tree, the proposed system generated answers that are both focused and explicitly specify
the case classification.
In addition, we measured the response time from input to the completion of answer
generation for each approach. The average response time (in seconds) of the proposed
system, ChatGPT, and Microsoft GraphRAG are 9.4, 11.1, and 25.9, respectively. Microsoft
GraphRAG also requires that a graph is constructed from the input documents before
answer generation, and this graph construction process took 4 min and 5.7 s. While the
proposed system runs substantially faster than GraphRAG, the comparison with ChatGPT
shows greater variability due to server load, even though the average was computed over
ten trials. The minimum response time of ChatGPT was 8.6 s, which is slightly faster than
that of the proposed system (8.9 s). Therefore, the response times of the proposed system
and ChatGPT can be considered comparable.
It is possible to improve the performance of both Microsoft GraphRAG and the
standard LLM to some extent by carefully specifying prompts and incorporating relevant
keywords into the queries. For example, in this case, the system may be queried with an
input such as: “In an audit of impairment of fixed assets, explain how assets can be grouped in
the presence of goodwill.” However, it would not be possible to include “goodwill” as part
of the query input if the user does not have prior knowledge of the fact that the presence of
goodwill affects case classification. Therefore, improving the quality of responses through
prompt engineering requires a high level of domain expertise. This prerequisite may render
the approach unsuitable for novice users, the expected users of the proposed system.

6. Limitations and Future Work
In Section 5, we focused on the “Decomposition of Actions and Ways” query pattern
and conducted experiments on this pattern. This query type constitutes a critical component
of the evaluation, as it requires exhaustive responses that fully cover multiple ways of action
decomposition. Among the three query patterns described in Section 3.3, the “Identifying
Purpose of Actions” and “Identifying Dependencies between Ways” patterns were not
examined experimentally in this study. A systematic evaluation of these two patterns lies
outside the scope of the present work and will be covered in future work.
As shown in Table 1, the proposed system does not achieve perfect accuracy under
the existing evaluation framework, indicating certain limitations of the current approach.
The accuracy of the system’s output is determined by two essential components: “identi-
fication of the query pattern” and “localization of the relevant node.” In the former, the
system demonstrates robust performance in determining the appropriate query type. For
example, the system can identify: What principles determine the boundaries of asset groups?
as “Decomposition of Action and Ways”, which includes abstract phrasing. In contrast,
localization of the relevant node remains challenging, particularly for queries that deviate
from the idealized form of queries used in Section 5. Since the system relies on keyword
extraction, the system may fail to associate a query with the correct node when the query
employs semantic equivalents, contains conceptual ambiguity, or is excessively short and
thus provides only a limited number of available keywords. As future work, we plan to
implement semantic retrieval methods, such as embedding-based searches, which may
allow the system to retrieve semantically related nodes even when the exact terminology
does not appear in the graph. This extension will enable the system to respond more
flexibly to diverse query expressions.
In addition, the functional categories defined in Section 3.1 are currently used only for
presentation purposes and are not yet fully integrated into the search mechanism. In future
work, we aim to enhance the search process by incorporating these functional categories
more robustly. For example, if a user query is related to “preventive measures”, the system
could insert a corresponding FILTER into the SPARQL query to restrict results to nodes
labeled with Prevention function.
A user study can be considered to evaluate the proposed system. As a concrete
example of system application, we will recruit novice students and divide them into two
groups. One group will use the proposed system and the other group will use ChatGPT to
solve tasks that require specialized knowledge. Then, we will analyze their performance
scores and assess their subjective experiences through questionnaires.
7. Conclusions
In this paper, we propose a system that provides users with goal-oriented knowledge
through a natural language interface. Using an RDF-converted function decomposition
tree, users can retrieve (1) finer-grained functions/actions, (2) goals/purposes, or (3) depen-
dencies of specified functions or actions. Such knowledge facilitates users’ understanding
of functions of artifacts or human actions and their performance of these actions. The
functional categories of the function decomposition tree, such as prevention functions and
counter functions, particularly clarify the teleological roles of the functions.
As shown in Section 5, we evaluated the core pattern, retrieving functions/actions.
Compared with other approaches, the proposed system provided only essential, directly
relevant knowledge. As reflected by the higher quantitative scores shown in Table 1, these
results demonstrate that our system can deliver responses that are both necessary and
sufficient for users’ needs.

In the structured approach to procedural knowledge extraction as proposed in this
paper, information is obtained from domain experts to construct the model, which increases
the cost but ensures accuracy. In unstructured methods such as ChatGPT and GraphRAG,
as we discussed in Section 1, unstructured natural language is the input to the system, and
necessary information is extracted based on the user’s query and accompanying prompt.
In contrast to the structured approach, relying on unstructured methods reduces the cost
but introduces limitations, including hallucinations and no guarantee of accuracy, as we
demonstrated in Section 5. Overall, there is a trade-off between accuracy and cost, and
the structured approach is particularly effective in domains where accuracy is critical,
such as for accounting audits. This study highlights the potential of structured knowledge
representations as a complementary alternative to unstructured language models, especially
in settings where reliability and user trust are paramount.
